High-Sensitivity Microbarometer Infrasound Transducer

A precision instrumentation architecture engineered for continuous sub-audible acoustic wave detection (0.01 – 20 Hz). The design integrates a physical two-port differential pressure transducer with an acoustic pneumatic RC filter, isolating infrasonic signatures of interest while mechanically suppressing ambient barometric drift and wind turbulence.

ΔP

Two-Port Differential Pressure Principle

Direct differential measurement across a custom differential transducer comparing active ambient pressure (Port A, PA) to a reference backing volume (Port B, PB).

ΔP(t) = PA(t) − PB(t)

Eliminates common-mode atmospheric baseline (~101.3 kPa) directly at the physical transducer, preventing ADC saturation and maximizing dynamic range for millipascal-level infrasound.

fc

Pneumatic RC High-Pass Filter

An acoustic high-pass filter formed by a precision micro-capillary bleed tube (acoustic resistance R) and sealed backing chamber (acoustic compliance C).

fc = 1 / (2πRC) ≈ 0.02 Hz

Sub-corner diurnal barometric swings passively equalize between ports, whereas infrasonic transients pass unattenuated into the differential sensing chamber.

V&V

Citable Signal & Realism Models

Standardized atmospheric acoustic profiles grounded in geophysics literature for rigorous validation and calibration:

  • Explosion transient: Friedlander blast shock impulse
  • Weather drift: Diurnal solar thermal/pressure ramp
  • Periodic / Helmholtz: Acoustic resonance modes
  • Wind / Broadband noise: 1/f atmospheric turbulence

Coupled with sensor realism injection (drift, thermal noise) and drift compensation with PSD characterization.

Physical Principles & Theory

Infrasound Microbarometer Physics

01

Two-Port Differential Pressure Principle

Ambient atmospheric pressure at sea level is approximately 101,325 Pa, with slow diurnal tidal oscillations and weather fronts creating ambient swings of ±500 to ±2,000 Pa. In contrast, infrasound signatures of interest (from explosions, volcanic eruptions, bolides, or severe weather) typically exhibit minute amplitudes from 0.001 Pa to 10 Pa.

Attempting to detect these millipascal perturbations using dual independent absolute pressure sensors and computing the difference digitally produces severe quantization error, sensor mismatch drift, and ADC dynamic range saturation.

ΔP(t) = PA(t) − PB(t)

A custom differential transducer core mechanically compares Port A directly against Port B, canceling out ~101.3 kPa of common-mode baseline pressure at the physical sensor level before amplification.

02

Pneumatic RC High-Pass Filter

Port A is coupled directly to the ambient atmosphere through a sintered porous filter cap. Port B is connected to a sealed reference chamber (backing volume) via a precision acoustic capillary bleed tube.

  • Acoustic Resistance (R): Viscous drag of air molecules inside the narrow capillary bleed tube, calculated using Poiseuille's law (Pa·s/m³).
  • Acoustic Compliance (C): Compressibility of air sealed in the rigid backing chamber (C = V / (ρ₀ · c₀²) in m³/Pa).
fc = 1 / (2πRC) ≈ 0.02 Hz
Hhp(jω) = jωRC / (1 + jωRC)

Slow barometric drift (< 0.02 Hz) equilibrates passively across the capillary tube, keeping ΔP ≈ 0. Fast infrasonic transients (0.05 to 20 Hz) cannot bleed through the capillary in time, creating a measurable pressure differential across the diaphragm.

03

Citable Atmospheric Signal Models

The simulator incorporates four reference wave models cited across atmospheric acoustics and geophysics literature:

1. Explosion Transient Friedlander (1946)

Blast shock wave propagation described by the classical modified Friedlander equation: P(t) = Ppeak · (1 − t/t+) · exp(−b · t/t+). Models high-energy abrupt positive overpressure followed by a prolonged sub-ambient rarefaction phase.

2. Weather Drift Band Bedard & Georges (2000)

Characterizes large-scale atmospheric gravity waves, mesoscale weather systems, and diurnal temperature ramps (< 0.005 Hz) that challenge sensor baseline stability, validating the rejection ratio of the pneumatic high-pass filter.

3. Periodic / Acoustic Cavity Goto & Johnson (2011)

Models monochromatic infrasound and harmonic resonance modes generated by open volcanic conduits, acoustic organ-pipe standing waves, and resonant cavity oscillations (0.1 – 5 Hz).

4. Broadband Wind Noise von Kármán (1948)

Simulates atmospheric boundary layer turbulence obeying the Kolmogorov / von Kármán power law spectrum with f−5/3 roll-off, injecting representative realistic wind noise across the passband.

04

Reading the Graphs

A practical guide to interpreting each live dashboard visualization, explaining the physical phenomenon it captures and why it matters to instrument evaluation:

1. Port A: PA(t) Raw Atmospheric Input

This plot displays the raw, unfiltered atmospheric pressure wave exactly as it arrives at the sensor's atmosphere-facing port, before any physical filtering occurs. It serves as the "ground truth" acoustic input driving the entire simulation, generated from one of four citable physical models based on the selected event type. Observing Port A allows you to see the true environmental disturbance in the air prior to entering the transducer. It provides the reference baseline against which all downstream filtering and sensing stages are compared.

2. Differential: ΔP(t) Pneumatic Sensor Output

This curve shows the actual sensor output—what you would measure on a physical instrument—after the pneumatic high-pass filter (capillary bleed tube and backing volume) removes slow pressure changes. The critical behavior to observe is frequency selectivity: for a rapid event like an explosion, the waveform matches Port A almost identically because fast infrasonic content passes straight through. Conversely, for a slow event like weather drift, the trace collapses toward flat zero because the gradual pressure change bleeds passively through the capillary. This plot provides direct visual proof of the two-port differential sensing principle successfully rejecting ambient barometric drift.

3. Bode Plot: Pneumatic High-Pass Theoretical Transfer Function

This graph illustrates the theoretical frequency response of the pneumatic filter itself, |Hhp(jω)|, derived from the transfer function Hhp(jω) = jωRC / (1 + jωRC). It represents the fixed physical behavior of the capillary resistance and backing volume, completely independent of whatever transient signal is currently generated. Crucially, this curve will not change when you switch event types or regenerate a signal, as it reflects the filter's intrinsic hardware characteristic rather than a live waveform. It updates exclusively when you adjust the physical acoustic resistance (R) or compliance (C) sliders, shifting the cutoff frequency fc.

4. Drift Compensation: Realism Layer Imperfections & DSP Firmware

This panel simulates real-world hardware imperfections by injecting electrical sensor noise alongside slow thermal pressure drift caused by temperature variations inside the sealed backing volume. It overlays three distinct traces to tell a before-and-after story: the clean theoretical signal, what a real imperfect sensor would actually output, and how closely a firmware-style digital filtering algorithm restores the true waveform. By demonstrating that slow baseline wander can be identified and stripped in software without distorting the underlying infrasound, this plot validates the project's "smart temperature compensation" engineering novelty. It proves that the sensor maintains high fidelity even during uncontrolled environmental temperature swings.

5. Noise Floor Spectrum: PSD Power Spectral Density

This power spectral density (PSD) plot measures how much residual noise energy exists at each frequency across the detection band, expressed in dB SPL. This represents the standard metrology protocol used in real infrasound instrumentation literature—including the Marcillo, Johnson & Hart (2012) reference paper—to quantify sensor limits. It provides the quantitative noise floor characterization (measured in mPa) that technical judges expect to evaluate when determining instrument sensitivity. A low, uniform noise floor across 0.01 to 20 Hz confirms that faint sub-audible signals will not be buried in electronic or acoustic self-noise.
